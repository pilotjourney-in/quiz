How the 1 in 60 rule works

The 1 in 60 rule says that if you are 1 NM off track after flying 60 NM, your track has drifted about 1° from the plan. It works because, for small angles, the angle in degrees is almost exactly 60 times the ratio of the side opposite to the distance flown. That lets you fix a navigation error in your head without a calculator.

When a fix shows you off track, two angles matter. The track error is how far your actual track has drifted from the planned one, based on the distance gone. The closing angle is the extra turn needed to converge on the destination, based on the distance still to go. Turn by the track error alone and you fly parallel to the planned track. Turn by both added together and you arrive over the destination.

Track error (TE) = 60 × distance off ÷ distance gone Closing angle (CA) = 60 × distance off ÷ distance to go Total correction = TE + CA (turn towards the planned track) Exact check: angle = atan(off ÷ distance)

Worked example

Flying from Delhi (VIDP) to Jaipur (VIJP), about 124 NM, you fix your position 4 NM right of track after 40 NM. TE = 60 × 4 ÷ 40 = 6°. With 84 NM to go, CA = 60 × 4 ÷ 84 ≈ 2.9°. The total correction is about 8.9°, so turn left roughly 9°: from a heading of 214° to about 205°. Exact trigonometry gives 8.4°, which shows how close the rule is.

The rule reads slightly high because it uses 60 where the true figure is 57.3, and the gap grows at larger angles. Above about 20°, use the exact value.

Sources: standard ATPL/DGCA General Navigation syllabus texts (1 in 60 rule); plane trigonometry for the exact values.
