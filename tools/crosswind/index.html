How it is calculated

Formulae used

  • Runway number × 10 is the heading, with 36 as 360°. You can override this with a published heading such as 281° for VIDP runway 28.
  • Angle = wind direction − heading, normalised to −180°…+180°.
  • Headwind = speed × cos(angle). A negative value is a tailwind.
  • Crosswind = speed × sin(angle). Positive is from the right; negative is from the left.
  • A gust re-runs the same two formulae at the gust speed.

See the crosswind component formula, with a worked example, in the DGCA CPL formula library.

How the crosswind component is found

A runway number is the magnetic heading of the centreline, rounded to the nearest ten degrees and written as two digits. Runway 28 means about 280 degrees, but at Indira Gandhi International Airport in Delhi the published heading for runway 28 is 281 degrees, so this calculator lets you type the exact figure when you have it. Wind in a METAR is reported in degrees true. Across most of India the magnetic variation is only about one degree, so the gap is small for exam practice, but in real flying you still convert the METAR wind to magnetic before you compare it with a runway heading.

The angle between the wind and the runway is wind direction minus heading, folded into the range from minus 180 to plus 180 degrees. The headwind is wind speed times the cosine of that angle. A negative result is a tailwind. The crosswind is wind speed times the sine of the angle. A positive sine means the wind is coming from the right of the nose; a negative sine means it is coming from the left. If you enter a gust, the same two formulae are run again with the gust speed so you can see the peak components as well as the sustained ones.

These figures are the same ones you would read from an E6B wind face or from a crosswind table in a training manual. They tell you whether a landing sits inside a typical training or aircraft limit, but they do not replace the aircraft flight manual, the ATIS or a current METAR. Use the diagram and the worked steps to check your own arithmetic, not to plan a real approach.
