Planning a descent with the 3° rule

Most airliner descents and instrument approaches follow a path of about 3°. The 1 in 60 rule turns that angle into easy numbers: 1° of path is about 100 ft of height for every nautical mile flown, so a 3° path loses about 300 ft per NM. Divide the height you need to lose by 300 and you get how many miles before the level-off point to start down. That point is your top of descent.

The rate of descent follows from your groundspeed. At 300 ft per NM and, say, 120 kt (2 NM a minute), you need 600 ft/min. The shortcut is ROD = 5 × groundspeed. That is why ATC and approach charts care about your groundspeed and not your indicated speed: a tailwind means a higher rate of descent.

Gradient (rule) ≈ 100 × angle ft/NM (3° → 300 ft/NM) Gradient (exact) = tan(angle) × 6,076 ft/NM (3° → 318 ft/NM) Descent distance = height to lose ÷ gradient Rate of descent = groundspeed × gradient ÷ 60 (3° → 5 × GS)

Worked example

Descending from 10,000 ft to 2,000 ft on a 3° path at 180 kt groundspeed: height to lose 8,000 ft. Rule of thumb: 8,000 ÷ 300 = 26.7 NM and 5 × 180 = 900 ft/min. Exact trigonometry gives 25.1 NM and 955 ft/min. Exam answers usually use the rule; the exact figure shows how far apart the two are.

On final approach, the same rule tells you the height to expect: 5 NM out on a 3° glide path you should be about 1,500 ft above the touchdown zone (exact 1,590 ft).

Sources: standard ATPL/DGCA General Navigation texts (1 in 60 rule); FAA Instrument Procedures Handbook (FAA-H-8083-16), descent planning and 3° glide path; ICAO Doc 8168 PANS-OPS (3° optimum final approach descent gradient).
